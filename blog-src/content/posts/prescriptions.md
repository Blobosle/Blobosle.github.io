# Out of love for the prescription

Prescriptions are how-to's, advice, a set of instructions. They originate in an attempt to achieve a certain objective in a domain, whether that be financial success, fashion sense, basketball shooting, and many more.

Some may perceive that the emergence of prescriptions come from the needs of people that want to "learn how-to do something". There is nothing wrong in believing that, except that when attempting to achieve something *significant* this trust in the effectiveness of prescriptions is what eventually turns against the person.

In this post, I will expand upon the uselessness of prescriptions when facing an uncertain environment, the domain dependence problem of not being able to distinguish the difference between learning, and understanding (that is classroom vs apprenticeship), and showing something related to this that I like to call density problems.

## Dying in the name of stability

Not a lot of people seem to like uncertainty, and with good reason (at least I feel like there is a good reason for this that you can derive on your own).

When it comes to stable environments, prescriptions are very convenient. It's easy to pick up a recipe and cook it, and the environments of the kitchen, while changing with context, can be imagined to be quite certain (you know where the frying pan is in your kitchen).

However, enter anything that has innate uncertainty and you can throw prescriptions out of the window, they will be useless. The issue with this is that most things that are worth doing in life have a high degree of uncertainty.

If, fresh out of college, you read a book about how to build a business, and try to apply the concepts, you will fail (change the book for a MBA for some interesting parallels). If you get a hold of a step-by-step guide by Tiger Woods on how to play golf just as he does, you will not become Tiger Woods. If you get a hold of a biography of Steve Jobs, you will never be able to have the same effectiveness as Steve Jobs himself did at Apple. In fact, even if Steve Jobs told his younger self a step-by-step on how to achieve the same level of success he had in the future, the younger self would not be able to achieve that level of success.

Weird claims thrown in the previous paragraph huh?

A lot of people like to hunt down for the secrets behind something, such as being successful, as if there is a dedicated mafia group controlling the mouths of every single successful person in the world in order to keep the secrets gate kept. Yet, why not consider the possibility that what is called a "secret" is not so much as secret as it is something that cannot be communicated.

How is knowledge passed on from person to person? I often make the distinction between *narratable,* and *non-narratable non-tractable knowledge*, for which the former can be effectively communicated through clear language, and the latter cannot be communicated or traced. When working with uncertainty it is the latter type of knowledge that will end up determining the outcome of the particular event.

You can approximate an understanding to non-tractable non-traceable non-narratable knowledge by associating it with the instinct that an expert or experienced individual may feel in their domain–that type of instinct, that when followed, produces peak performance. You may also think about it like that feeling that you get when you can't justify your decision-making to another person, but you know deep down that you are right.

It is the innate property of this type of knowledge that it is un-transmissible, and, coupled with its impact in the outcome of an activity, it is the reason why we can't to-do's that work for everything. It is also one of the more obscured rules of the game of life.

## Make the stars align, all the time, every time

The way that non-tractable knowledge is acquired is through exposure to the domain of the activity. This mean that if you want to build a successful business, you quite literally, build a business to learn how to build, instead of learning from a degree, MBA, or book. If you want to learn to program, you don't watch hour long tutorials of a person trying to explain a whole programming language in a condensed manner, you build projects related to the programming language you are learning, because your success is literally determined by your capacity to build using that language.

You learn with the domain itself, anything else causes deviations. This is also the reason why people straight out of college fail to adapt to the work environment in comparison to those coming out from an apprenticeship with an experienced individual: the student learns how to perform well in classes that happen to be of the domain, they don't learn the domain itself. The student becomes good at finishing school programming projects, yet struggles when they need to ship a program under a similar deadline.

Does this mean that we should throw college, books, and all other forms of prescriptions away? No, they just get relegated to the second order. It is only after exposure is gained that you can benefit from how-to's–the more exposure the better. Even, in the case of college, the deviations from the domain itself may not be that far off (after all the classes are related to the domain), as such in the long term, the graduated student can gain the exposure necessary in a shorter period of time.

The readjustment is a downturn in terms of progress. It can be seen like a recessions in an economy. A necessary period of readjustment that will only be required to be done once at best ("best" does a lot of heavy lifting).

Also because of its nature, it is impossible (maybe) to ascertain its existence to explain in a medium like writing. As such, funnily enough, I will ask the reader to obtain exposure to gain the non-narratable non-traceable non-tractable knowledge to understand non-narratable non-traceable non-tractable knowledge. You gain understanding of a domain through the domain itself.
